The Fiscal Cliff Calendar

Actually Unprecedented

Cutting through the word "historic"
The chyrons call everything "historic." Most of it isn't — blowing past the deadline, stopgap funding, and giant catch-all bills have been routine for three decades. But a handful of things in this stretch genuinely broke records or precedent. Here's what earns the word.
Record · Shutdown
43 Days
The fall 2025 shutdown was the longest in U.S. history, breaking the prior record of 35 days set in 2018–19.
Rare · Second Lapse
76-Day DHS Shutdown
A second, department-specific shutdown in the same cycle pushed Homeland Security funding to Apr. 30, 2026 — extraordinarily long for a single-agency lapse.
First in History
A Speaker Ousted
The 2023 motion to vacate that removed Speaker McCarthy was the first successful one ever in the House — a threat that now shadows every deal.
Constitutional Test
The Power of the Purse
Escalating use of rescissions and deferrals is testing the 1974 Impoundment Control Act — who controls money once Congress appropriates it — like nothing since the law passed.
Structural
Razor Majorities
A House majority of only a handful of seats leaves leadership almost no votes to spare — the July stopgap squeaked through 220–205 — making regular-order appropriations close to impossible.
Most in 26+ Years
69 Heading Out
Sixty-nine members aren't seeking re-election — the most since at least 2000 and 32% above 2024, draining dealmaking experience. See the next tab.
Not actually historic: missing the Oct. 1 deadline (the norm since FY1997), funding by continuing resolution, packaging bills into omnibuses, and shutdowns themselves (1980, 1995–96, 2013, 2018–19). The dysfunction is the baseline — only the intensity is new.

A Shutdown & You

What actually stops for the public
Skip the politics — here's the direct, everyday impact if funding lapses and the government shuts down. Find yourself below.
If you fly
TSA screeners and air-traffic controllers are forced to work without pay. As absences climb, security lines and flight delays grow — 1,000+ TSA officers quit during the 76-day DHS shutdown.
If you get SNAP or WIC
Food aid is at risk. In the 2025 shutdown, roughly 42 million people faced losing full SNAP benefits, and WIC support for mothers and infants ran short.
If you're a federal worker or contractor
Around 2 million federal workers are furloughed or work unpaid. Employees are owed back pay by law; contractors usually get nothing.
If you need a federal loan
New Small Business Administration loans and new farm loans freeze, and no new federal contracts are signed — a direct hit to small businesses and growers.
If you're visiting a park or museum
National parks and the Smithsonian close or go unstaffed; trash, restrooms, and rangers disappear even where gates stay open.
If you need a passport or IRS help
Passport and visa processing can slow, and IRS phone lines and taxpayer help largely go dark — refunds and paperwork back up.
What keeps coming: Social Security and Medicare payments, VA medical care, the U.S. mail, and active-duty troops on the job — all funded outside the annual bills. The catch: many of the people delivering those services work without a paycheck until the shutdown ends.

What "Retiring" Pays

Benefits after Congress
The persistent myth is that members collect their full salary — and free health care — for life. They don't. What they actually get is the standard federal-employee package: a formula pension they pay into, a 401(k)-style match, Social Security, and the option (not a gift) to keep buying federal health coverage.

The pension is formula-based — roughly 1% of a member's highest three-year average salary for each year served — so it rewards longevity, not a single term. The average congressional pension paid in 2023 was about $45,000 a year; someone who serves only the five-year minimum gets far less. Marjorie Taylor Greene's roughly five years, for example, pencils out to about $8,700 a year — and not until she turns 62. Two footnotes on leaving: ex-members face a lobbying "cooling-off" period (two years for senators, one for representatives), and leftover campaign cash can't be pocketed — it can only move to other committees, charities, or a leadership PAC.
